# Ceramics Decarbonization Pathways (Europe, 2026)

Ceramics are made by shaping non-metallic inorganic materials such as clay and then drying and firing them. Firing temperatures run from about 450°C for some technical ceramics to over 1,800°C for refractories, and most of the sector's CO2 comes from these drying and firing steps. [JRC Science for Policy Brief JRC144122 (2026), pp. 2–3](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC144122/JRC144122_01.pdf) A smaller share comes from the clay itself: carbonates such as limestone or dolomite in the raw materials release CO2 during firing. [Cerame-Unie, Ceramic Roadmap to 2050, p. 34](https://www.ceramicroadmap2050.eu/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/ceramic-roadmap-to-2050.pdf) Clean heat can deal with the first part. Only changes to the clay body or carbon capture can deal with the second.

This page draws on the European Commission's Joint Research Centre (JRC) and Cerame-Unie, the European ceramic industry association. Cerame-Unie's roadmap was published in 2021, so its figures are older than the JRC's. Industry-body figures are the association's own. For the JRC's short summary of emerging technologies across hard-to-abate sectors, see [EU JRC Factsheets on Emerging Technologies for Hard-to-Abate Industries (2026)](eu-emerging-technologies-hard-to-abate-industries-2026.md).

## Sector Profile

- **Size**: in 2023 the EU ceramic industry had a turnover of €26 billion and produced 8.3 Mt of goods. It has close to 20,000 companies and about 200,000 direct jobs, exports more than a third of its production outside the EU and has a trade surplus of €5.1 billion. [JRC144122, pp. 1–2](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC144122/JRC144122_01.pdf)
- **Products**: Cerame-Unie recognises nine sectors. Wall and floor tiles, bricks and roof tiles, and vitrified clay pipes make up the bulk of production, and together with sanitaryware they supply the construction industry. [JRC144122, p. 1](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC144122/JRC144122_01.pdf)
- **Many small plants**: the 2024 EU ETS operator list includes 529 ceramic installations, which reported 8.41 Mt of verified CO2. By comparison, 195 cement clinker installations reported 81.9 Mt. On average the largest ceramic plants in the EU ETS emit 26 times less CO2 than clinker plants, which the JRC says affects the industry's ability to decarbonise. [JRC144122, p. 3](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC144122/JRC144122_01.pdf) Cerame-Unie puts ceramics at 10% of industrial installations in the EU ETS but only 1% of its industrial emissions. [Ceramic Roadmap to 2050, p. 30](https://www.ceramicroadmap2050.eu/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/ceramic-roadmap-to-2050.pdf)

## Emissions Baseline

- **Total**: EU ceramic production caused around 19 Mt of CO2 in 2019, about 1% of EU greenhouse gas emissions. [JRC144122, p. 2](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC144122/JRC144122_01.pdf) Cerame-Unie's 19 Mt total includes indirect emissions from electricity, so it is not directly comparable with the 8.41 Mt of verified direct emissions from EU ETS installations above. [Ceramic Roadmap to 2050, p. 30](https://www.ceramicroadmap2050.eu/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/ceramic-roadmap-to-2050.pdf)
- **Trend**: total emissions fell by around 33% between 1990 and 2019 and by more than 45% from their peak in the 2000s, mainly through process optimisation, efficiency and fuel substitution. The biggest single cut came in the 1980s, when the sector switched almost entirely from coal to natural gas. [Ceramic Roadmap to 2050, p. 31](https://www.ceramicroadmap2050.eu/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/ceramic-roadmap-to-2050.pdf) The JRC notes that production and CO2 emissions have decoupled since 2010. [JRC144122, p. 3](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC144122/JRC144122_01.pdf)
- **Where emissions come from**: around 90% of emissions come from three sectors: bricks and roof tiles, wall and floor tiles, and refractories. [Ceramic Roadmap to 2050, p. 30](https://www.ceramicroadmap2050.eu/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/ceramic-roadmap-to-2050.pdf)

Share of emission sources in the European ceramic industry, 2020:

| Source | Share |
| --- | --- |
| Fuel combustion for drying and firing | 64% |
| Indirect emissions, mainly from electricity production | 19% |
| Process emissions from the mineral transformation of clay | 17% |

Source: [Cerame-Unie, Ceramic Roadmap to 2050, p. 30](https://www.ceramicroadmap2050.eu/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/ceramic-roadmap-to-2050.pdf).

The split varies a lot by product. Process emissions are 30% of total emissions in bricks and roof tiles and can reach 60% in clay blocks. [Ceramic Roadmap to 2050, p. 30](https://www.ceramicroadmap2050.eu/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/ceramic-roadmap-to-2050.pdf) They amounted to 3.34 Mt CO2 in 2019 and depend on local geology. Cerame-Unie argues that relocating plants to lower-carbonate clays would not be environmentally sound, because local raw materials avoid long-distance transport. [Ceramic Roadmap to 2050, p. 34](https://www.ceramicroadmap2050.eu/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/ceramic-roadmap-to-2050.pdf)

## Decarbonization Levers

The JRC groups technologies into five levers. The abatement potentials are upper bounds for each lever and cannot be added up.

| Lever | Example technologies | Abatement potential | Readiness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alternative materials | Circular economy, optimised raw materials | Up to 18% | High |
| Waste heat and other efficiency | CHP, thermal storage, insulation, digitalisation | Up to 20% | High |
| Innovative drying and firing | Heat pumps, hybrid kilns, electrode-based and induction heating, microwave drying and heating | Up to 63% | Medium |
| Alternative fuels | Biomethane, hydrogen, electric kilns and electrification | Up to 82% | High |
| Negative emissions | Carbon capture, including for use | Up to 100% | Low |

Source: [JRC144122, Table 4](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC144122/JRC144122_01.pdf). The JRC counts hydrogen, rapid and infrared dryers, microwave firing and several CO2 separation technologies as emerging. Efficiency improvements and best available techniques are already commercial. [JRC144122, p. 4](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC144122/JRC144122_01.pdf)

- **Energy supply, not technology, is the bottleneck.** The JRC concludes that the transition depends on a supply of affordable decarbonised energy (biogas, hydrogen or electricity) more than on technology readiness. [JRC144122, p. 1](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC144122/JRC144122_01.pdf)
- **Electrification**: in its 2021 roadmap Cerame-Unie said electric kilns were considered economically unsound in most ceramic sectors, because electricity costs much more than natural gas and because ceramics is not eligible for indirect cost compensation under the EU ETS State Aid Guidelines. [Ceramic Roadmap to 2050, p. 37](https://www.ceramicroadmap2050.eu/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/ceramic-roadmap-to-2050.pdf) Since then, industrial-scale electric tunnel kilns have started up in Austria (see below).
- **Security of supply**: kilns run 24 hours a day, seven days a week, and an unplanned interruption in fuel supply can severely damage them. This matters for any switch to hydrogen, syngas or biomass. [Ceramic Roadmap to 2050, p. 37](https://www.ceramicroadmap2050.eu/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/ceramic-roadmap-to-2050.pdf)
- **Process emissions**: Cerame-Unie lists fewer carbon-containing additives, lower-carbon clay mixes, using less raw material for the same product, and new technologies. It says process emissions cannot be avoided completely at a competitive cost and would probably need to be offset by biogenic methods. [Ceramic Roadmap to 2050, p. 34](https://www.ceramicroadmap2050.eu/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/ceramic-roadmap-to-2050.pdf)
- **Carbon capture** is expected only from 2040, and research on it within the ceramic industry is limited. Small, scattered plants make capture harder to scale and connect to infrastructure, even though they ease local sourcing of materials and fuels. [JRC144122, pp. 1, 5–6](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC144122/JRC144122_01.pdf)

## Trajectories and Costs

| Trajectory | 2030 | 2040 | 2050 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cerame-Unie Roadmap 2050 | −64% | −80% | −100% |
| JRC POTEnCIA CETO 2024 scenario | −34% | −82% | −91% |

Source: [JRC144122, Table 2](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC144122/JRC144122_01.pdf), total emission abatement against a 2000 reference year.

In Cerame-Unie's roadmap, switching to renewable energy (low-carbon hydrogen, biofuels and decarbonised electricity) cuts emissions from fuel combustion and indirect emissions by about 60%. Innovations can address close to 50% of process emissions, and capture, carbon removal and offsetting up to 40%. [JRC144122, pp. 3–4](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC144122/JRC144122_01.pdf)

- **Investment**: the Cerame-Unie roadmap implies investments of up to €1.2 billion a year in 2050 and €27 billion in total up to 2050. [JRC144122, p. 4](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC144122/JRC144122_01.pdf)
- **Production cost**: POTEnCIA indicates a total production cost of €60/t by 2050, which the JRC describes as a 1.8% increase on current levels. CO2 abatement costs in the industry remain confidential. [JRC144122, p. 4](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC144122/JRC144122_01.pdf)
- **2030 gap**: by 2019 the industry had cut CO2 by 33% against 1990. Reaching −57% in 2030 means cutting a further 6.8 Mt, or 0.61 Mt a year (35% against 2019). That is faster than the 0.45 Mt a year achieved in 2010–2019, but slower than the 1.1 Mt a year of 2000–2010. Production may also grow 11.5% between 2019 and 2030. [JRC144122, p. 6](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC144122/JRC144122_01.pdf)

## Industrial Demonstrations

The JRC counts 39 ceramic-related demonstrators in the EU. Most focus on waste heat recovery and efficiency (13), alternative fuels (11) and innovative drying and firing (9). Spain hosts 14 and Italy 11, with the rest in Austria, France, Germany, Greece, the Netherlands and Portugal. Only two Innovation Fund projects directly address ceramics: Mitigat and CT Quarry. [JRC144122, pp. 5–6](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC144122/JRC144122_01.pdf)

| Project | Segment and site | Configuration | Reported result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Roca Group / LAUFEN electric tunnel kiln | Sanitaryware, Gmunden (Austria) | World's first electric tunnel kiln for sanitaryware, developed with Keramischer Ofenbau; replaced a gas-fired kiln shut down in early 2024 | Needs a third of the energy for the same firing output; about 5,000 t CO2 a year less than the gas kiln. Roca has about 100 gas-fired tunnel kilns worldwide. [LAUFEN](https://www.laufen.com/news-stories/world-premiere-laufen-pioneers-with-first-co2-free-tunnel-kiln) |
| Wienerberger GreenBricks (NEFI) | Bricks, Uttendorf (Austria) | World's largest industrial electric kiln, opened 28 November 2024; sawdust tested instead of coal or paper fibres in clay mixes | About 90% lower production emissions and a third less energy on green electricity; about €30 million invested in the kiln; test operation at 270 t of bricks a day. [Wienerberger (2024)](https://www.wienerberger.com/en/media/press-releases/2024/20241129-wienerberger-starts-Europes-greenest-brick-production.html) |
| eLITHE (Horizon Europe) | Three pilot sites | Electrode and induction frit smelter, microwave alumina calciner, and a hybrid brick tunnel kiln burning electricity and hydrogen; 18 partners, January 2024 to December 2027, €11.2 million EU contribution | Targets over 97,000 t CO2 and over 505 GWh of natural gas a year avoided for a full-scale unit replaced. [CORDIS 101138325](https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/101138325) |
| CT Quarry (Innovation Fund) | Recycled raw materials, Almería (Spain) | Plant to turn over 100,000 t a year of sludge from Cosentino's agglomerated stone production into over 247,000 t a year of secondary raw materials for construction, glass and ceramics; €36.7 million grant | Expects 62% lower GHG emissions than the reference and about 1.04 Mt CO2e avoided; operation planned from 30 June 2030. [CINEA project factsheet 101191307](https://ec.europa.eu/assets/cinea/project_fiches/innovation_fund/101191307.pdf) |
| CCS4CER | Ceramic tiles, Emilia-Romagna (Italy) | Regional project (2024–2026) assessing CO2 capture with molten carbonate fuel cells and mineralisation of tile production waste; industrial partners include Panariagroup, Ascot and Sacmi | Research stage; a technology report is planned at the end. [Romagna Tech](https://romagnatech.eu/en/progetti/ccs4cer-decarbonisation-and-sustainability-for-the-ceramics-industry/) |

Wienerberger, the market leader in bricks and roof tiles, accounted for over 10% of the sector's verified emissions in 2024. Few companies publish decarbonisation pledges; the JRC names Wienerberger, Roca Group, NGK Insulators, CoorsTek Technical Ceramics, RHI Magnesita and Sto. [JRC144122, p. 5](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC144122/JRC144122_01.pdf)

## Policy Context

- **EU ETS**: the JRC calls it the most relevant policy with direct influence on ceramic decarbonisation so far. [JRC144122, p. 3](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC144122/JRC144122_01.pdf) For the 2026 review proposal, see [EU Carbon Market Reform](../policies/eu-carbon-market-reform-2026.md).
- **CBAM**: ceramics are **not** CBAM goods. CBAM currently applies to cement, iron and steel, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity and hydrogen. [European Commission, CBAM](https://taxation-customs.ec.europa.eu/carbon-border-adjustment-mechanism_en) See [EU CBAM: Definitive Period from 2026](../policies/eu-cbam-definitive-period-2026.md).
- **Funding**: the JRC argues that the wide range of products and processes spreads research funding thin, and that the transition needs a stable legal framework, financial support and access to competitive decarbonised energy. [JRC144122, p. 6](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC144122/JRC144122_01.pdf)

## Practical Takeaways

- **Start with the product's emission split.** For tiles and sanitaryware, fuel is the main target. For clay blocks, process CO2 can be up to 60% of the total, so clean heat alone will not be enough.
- **Electric firing has left the lab.** Industrial electric tunnel kilns now run for sanitaryware and bricks in Austria, both at plants with on-site solar and green electricity.
- **Power price and grid capacity decide the case.** The JRC and Cerame-Unie both point to energy cost and supply, not technology, as the main constraint.
- **Plan capture as a 2040s option.** The JRC does not expect carbon capture in ceramics before 2040, and small plants make it harder to connect to CO2 transport and storage.

## Open Questions

- Will the sector meet its 2030 trajectory, given that it needs a faster cut than in 2010–2019 while output may grow?
- How can scattered, small plants reach CO2 transport and storage, or will residual process emissions mainly be handled through clay reformulation and removals?
- Will Cerame-Unie update its 2021 roadmap now that industrial electric kilns are operating?
